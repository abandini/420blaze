Calculator + Real Numbers

Edible Dosage Calculator

How much THC ends up in each brownie, gummy or cookie you make at home, the math behind it, and what controlled studies actually saw at 10, 25 and 50 mg.

By Blazin Bill · cannabis writer, not a clinician · Published November 27, 2026

TL;DR, the edible dosage formula: grams of flower × label Total THC % × 10 gives the mg on the label. Multiply by an infusion efficiency of 0.576–0.863 (measured from flower label to finished oil, decarb losses included), then divide by the number of servings. 1 g of 20% flower ends up with roughly 115–173 mg in the batch, or 7.2–10.8 mg per brownie when you cut 16. Homemade potency is always an estimate. Nobody lab-tests your kitchen, so plan around the high number.

Edible dosage calculator

Pick where you’re starting from and fill in what you know. The numbers update as you type. The math runs in your browser. There’s no body-weight box, and the section on body weight below explains why.

How do you calculate THC in homemade edibles?

You multiply your way from the label to the plate. Start with how much THC the flower holds, take off what the decarb and the infusion lose, and split what’s left by the number of pieces. Here’s the whole calculation, the same one the calculator runs and the same one our cannabutter ratio calculator uses, so the two pages agree:

The edible dosage formula, step by step

grams × Total THC % × 10 = mg of THC on the label × infusion efficiency (0.576 / 0.75 / 0.863) = mg in the batch, decarb losses included (skipped the oven? This model can’t estimate that batch; decarb first) ÷ number of servings = mg per serving

Worked example, 1 g of flower labeled 20% total THC, then decarbed: 1 × 20 × 10 = 200 mg × 0.75 = 150 mg in the batch ÷ 16 brownies = 9.4 mg per brownie (7.2 to 10.8 mg across the low and high assumptions). Actual potency can be higher or lower than this range; it is a model, not a measurement.

Each factor has a source and a range. None of them is exact for your kitchen. A chemistry aside: in a lab study, a cannabis extract heated in a vacuum oven at 230°F (110°C) lost 7.94% of its total THC during decarb [3]. We don’t subtract that on top, because the pharmacy efficiency numbers already include decarb and extraction losses. Subtracting it twice would make your batch look weaker than it probably is.

Automating the dosing math in a spreadsheet

If you bake often, you can automate ingredient dosing with one spreadsheet row. Put grams in column A, Total THC % in B, servings in C, and use =A2*B2*10*0.75/C2 for mg per serving. Swap 0.75 for 0.863 to see the high estimate, which is the one to plan around. To go the other way, from a target mg per serving in D to grams of flower, use =D2*C2/(B2*10*0.863), which sizes the batch from the high-end estimate. Both formulas assume the flower was decarbed in the oven first.

Three things stand out. A gram in a 16-piece pan comes in under 10 mg per brownie at the middle estimate, but the high estimate (10.8 mg) tips just past the 10 mg adult-use serving cap in Ohio and Michigan. An eighth in the same pan puts every brownie at two and a half to nearly four times that cap. The cheapest dial you have is the knife. Actual potency can be higher or lower than this range; it is a model, not a measurement.

One more thing the math can’t fix: these numbers assume the THC is spread evenly through the batter. We found no published study of how evenly it spreads in a home pan, so stir the butter in thoroughly. What is a standard edible dose?

In regulated markets, “a serving” is a legal limit, not a medical recommendation. The adult-use per-serving cap in Ohio and Michigan is 10 mg of THC. Ohio medical allows 55 mg per serving and Michigan medical 50 mg per dose. Florida has no adult-use market (our Florida marijuana laws guide explains why), and its medical program caps edibles at 10 mg per serving.

The one regulator figure aimed at consumers rather than products comes from Canada. Health Canada’s advice is to “look for products that contain 2.5 mg of THC or less” [9]. You’ll also hear “start with 5.” That traces to “First Time 5,” a 2014 Colorado industry education campaign that told newcomers to take half of the state’s 10 mg serving [29]. It was an industry campaign, not a state rule.

None of these numbers is a recommendation from us, and we’re not going to pick a dose for you. They’re the reference points regulators use, and the calculator’s colors measure your serving against them.

On a 0–100 “drug effect” scale, people rated placebo 2.3, 10 mg 18.0, 25 mg 38.3 and 50 mg 47.5. Average heart rate went from 70.4 beats per minute on placebo to 76.2, 80.3 and 83.2 [10]. The table doesn’t say whether those means are peak values or averages over the session, so read them as a trend.

Two cautions on reading the chart. First, results at 10 mg differ by study: capsules in one study produced no cardiovascular or cognitive effects and only mild side effects, while a brownie in another was clearly felt. Different people, products and meals give different results. Second, the brownie study’s 10 mg result says nothing about driving. “Didn’t impair a lab test” is not “fine to drive.”

What about regular users? A systematic review found that people who use cannabis regularly feel less from the same dose. Tolerance was strongest for effects on thinking and memory, and weaker for intoxication and heart effects [23]. We found no study that turns that into a reliable multiplier, so the calculator doesn’t offer a “tolerance” setting.

No known lethal dose is not the same as harmless

In the research we checked, no lethal dose of THC has been established in people; that is not the same as harmless. At one Colorado hospital from 2014 to 2016, edibles accounted for 10.7% of the visits attributed to cannabis, while making up only 0.32% of Colorado’s cannabis sales by THC weight. Edible visits were more often for intoxication (48% vs. 28%), psychiatric symptoms (18.0% vs. 10.9%) and heart symptoms (8.0% vs. 3.1%) than visits from inhaled cannabis [21]. The authors also note that the only Colorado deaths definitively tied to cannabis involved edibles: a man who jumped from a balcony, a woman killed by her husband after he ate an edible, and a suicide [21]. Separately, rare case reports link high-potency cannabis to fatal heart rhythm problems [31].

Kids are the other risk. Reported edible exposures in children under 6 went from 207 in 2017 to 3,054 in 2021. Of all cases, 97.7% happened at a residence, and 22.7% of the children were admitted to the hospital [22]. Homemade brownies look exactly like regular brownies. Label them, lock them up, and keep them out of reach of kids and pets.

If a child may have eaten one, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 right away, even without symptoms, or 911 in an emergency [13]. General warning signs (not clinical guidance, and not a complete list): get emergency help for anyone with chest pain, fainting, trouble breathing, a seizure, unresponsiveness, severe confusion, or thoughts of self-harm. If you have a heart condition or take prescription medicines, talk to a clinician before using edibles.

Does body weight change edible dosing?

Not according to any evidence we could find. People search for an “edible dosage calculator by weight” because it sounds logical: bigger body, bigger dose. But we found no study showing that body weight predicts how strongly an edible hits you. Every reference point on this page uses fixed milligrams: the state serving caps, Health Canada’s 2.5 mg, and the expert protocol’s 1 mg and 2.5 mg starting doses [5][9][12]. At least one popular calculator (Leafly’s) asks for your weight, but it doesn’t publish how it uses it [26].

What does seem to matter more:

  • Tolerance. Regular users feel less from the same dose [23].
  • Food. A high-fat meal raised total THC exposure and pushed the peak later in a controlled capsule study [14]. A brownie is itself a fatty food.
  • Individual metabolism. THC is broken down in the gut wall and the liver. In a lab study of human intestinal tissue, the gut-wall step ran mainly through an enzyme called CYP2C9 [24], and the brownie researchers describe “considerable inter-individual variability” in how people absorb and respond to oral cannabis [10].
  • Sex. In the same capsule study, women had higher peak THC levels than men after 5 mg on an empty stomach [14].

That’s a reason to skip the weight box, not proof that weight never matters. If a study ever shows it does, we’ll add it.

How long do edibles take to kick in and how long do they last?

Longer than smoking on both counts. In the Johns Hopkins brownie studies, effects didn’t show up until 30–60 minutes after eating, peaked at 1.5–3 hours, and lasted 6–8 hours [10][11]. Public-health agencies give wider windows because they’re describing real-world products and people.

Food shifts the clock. In a controlled study of THC capsules, taking them after a high-fat meal raised total exposure to THC and its active metabolite and delayed the peak [14]. In practice, a brownie after a big dinner may come on later and stretch out longer than one on a light stomach. The brownie researchers put it plainly: the slow onset “makes dose titration more difficult and increases the chances of acute overdose” [10]. A common way people get into trouble is eating a second piece because the first one “isn’t working” yet.

“How long do edibles last” is not the same as “how long are they in your system.” After a single 10–50 mg brownie, THC was detectable in whole blood for up to about 22 hours [11]. In another study, urine collected over the 8 hours after a 10 or 25 mg brownie tested positive under federal workplace cutoffs in 97.6% of sessions, compared with 59.5% after vaping 5 or 20 mg [16]. So there’s no sign that edibles clear your system faster. Regular use is widely reported to extend detection, but we haven’t found a primary source for the day counts you see online, so we won’t quote them.

Why edible calculators are estimates

Because even the professionals miss. In a 2015 study of 75 edible products from 47 brands bought in San Francisco, Los Angeles and Seattle, only 17% had THC within ±10% of the label. 60% contained meaningfully less THC than labeled and 23% contained more [17]. Those were medical-dispensary products before modern state testing. Today Ohio requires packages to land at 90–110% of target [5], Michigan allows servings to vary ±15% [28] and Florida allows up to 15% [7]. Even a legal 10 mg gummy can be 11 mg. Our post Why “10mg” Isn’t 10mg digs into that drift.

A home batch has none of that testing, and every factor in the formula is a range:

  • No home study exists. We found no peer-reviewed lab measurement of homemade cannabutter or brownie potency, or of how evenly THC spreads through a pan. That’s a real gap in the research.
  • The infusion range is from olive oil. The 57.6–86.3% is a range across study groups of pharmacy-made oils with lab checks, not individual batches [4]. Experts quoted in the press estimated 40–60% for dairy butter, and one put it closer to 30%, but those are opinions, not measurements [25].
  • Your oven isn’t a pharmacy oven. The efficiency range comes from pharmacy labs with controlled heating steps. In a 2026 lab study, measured cannabinoid losses appeared at low temperatures too (6–8% at 140–194°F, 60–90°C, over two-hour holds) and became substantial from about 250°F (120°C), growing with time and temperature [30]. Those are CBD figures from hemp flower in a lab, not THC in a home oven, but an oven that runs hot, or a long bake, can cost you more.
  • The label is a sample. The THC percent on your flower comes from testing a sample, not your exact gram.

Put together, the low and high ends of this calculator differ by about 1.5 times. That’s why it shows a range and sizes servings from the high-end estimate. Underestimate the batch and every piece is stronger than you planned. Actual potency can be higher or lower than this range; it is a model, not a measurement. Why edibles hit harder than smoking

Three things change when THC goes through your stomach instead of your lungs.

Less THC gets in. In a classic study, only 6 ± 3% of THC eaten in a chocolate cookie reached the bloodstream, compared with 18 ± 6% from smoking [20]. Absorption was “slow and erratic.”

More of it becomes 11-hydroxy-THC. Before swallowed THC reaches your blood, your gut wall and liver break much of it down, and one of the products is a metabolite called 11-OH-THC. In a lab study of human intestinal tissue, that gut-wall step ran mainly through an enzyme called CYP2C9 [24]. After a THC brownie, blood levels of 11-OH-THC were similar to THC itself [11]. In a 2023 trial with a 20 mg THC brownie, total 11-OH-THC exposure was about 1.2 times THC exposure [18]. Smoking produces relatively less of the metabolite [19].

That metabolite is at least as potent. A 2026 analysis pooled 112 infrequent users from seven controlled trials. Per unit in the blood, 11-OH-THC was about 1.4 times as potent as THC for the subjective high but topped out at a lower maximum effect, and about 5.4 times as potent for raising heart rate [19]. That paper appears to be a preprint, not yet peer reviewed, so treat it as preliminary.

You’ll often read that 11-OH-THC is “two to three times more potent” than THC. We looked for the source of that figure and found none. “At least as potent, possibly more” is as far as the evidence goes.

Timing is the other difference. In a controlled vaping and smoking study, blood THC and heart rate peaked within 30 minutes and were back near baseline in 3–4 hours, though some effects on mood and thinking lasted up to 6 hours on average [27]. An edible takes 30–60 minutes just to start. Inhaling gives you feedback in minutes, which is why some people prefer it. Our best dry herb vaporizers guide covers that route. And one more surprise from the 2023 trial: adding 640 mg of CBD to a 20 mg THC brownie made the effects stronger, not milder [18]. That is far more CBD than a typical CBD product holds, so it doesn’t settle every case, but it’s the controlled edible evidence we found. Don’t count on CBD to take the edge off.

Edible dosage FAQ

How many mg of THC is in 1 gram of weed?

Multiply the Total THC percent on the label by 10. A gram of 20% flower carries 200 mg of THC on the label. After an oven decarb and infusion into butter or oil, our estimate is that 115 to 173 mg ends up in the batch, about 150 mg in the middle. That is an estimate, not a lab result. [1][4]

How much THC is in one weed brownie?

Divide the THC in the batch by the number of pieces. With 1 gram of flower labeled 20% total THC, then decarbed, in a pan cut into 16, each brownie lands at roughly 7.2 to 10.8 mg. With 3.5 grams in the same pan, it is roughly 25 to 38 mg each. Uneven mixing can make some squares stronger than others.

What is a good starting dose for edibles?

We are writers, not clinicians, so we can't pick a dose for you. For reference, Health Canada tells consumers to look for products with 2.5 mg of THC or less, and 10 mg is the adult-use per-serving cap in Ohio and Michigan. In one controlled study, 10 mg was clearly felt by people who had not used cannabis for at least 60 days. [9][5][6][10]

Should I dose edibles by body weight?

We found no evidence that body weight predicts how hard an edible hits. State serving caps, Health Canada and an expert dosing consensus all use fixed milligrams. Tolerance, food in your stomach and individual metabolism appear to matter more, which is why this calculator has no weight box. [12][14][23]

How long should I wait before eating more of an edible?

Longer than feels natural. In controlled studies, effects started 30 to 60 minutes after eating and peaked 1.5 to 3 hours after eating. Health Canada says it can take up to 4 hours to feel the full effects. Eating more before the first dose peaks is a common way people end up far past their plan. [10][9]

Why do edibles feel stronger than smoking?

When you eat THC, your gut and liver break much of it down into 11-hydroxy-THC, which research suggests is at least as potent as THC. After an edible, blood levels of 11-OH-THC run about equal to THC, and in one brownie trial total 11-OH-THC exposure was about 1.2 times THC exposure. Effects also last longer, about 6 to 8 hours in studies. The popular claim that 11-OH-THC is “2 to 3 times more potent” than THC has no source we could find; a 2026 preprint put it at about 1.4 times for the subjective high, with a lower ceiling. [11][18][19]
