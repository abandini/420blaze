Calculator + Real Numbers

Cannabutter Ratio Calculator

How much flower goes in a stick of butter, what that does to every tablespoon, and which popular rules of thumb have no source behind them.

By Blazin Bill · cannabis writer, not a clinician · Published November 27, 2026

TL;DR, the cannabutter ratio rule: every gram of flower holds 10 mg of THC for each point of Total THC on the label, so 1 g of 20% flower carries 200 mg. Across study groups of pharmacy-made cannabis olive oils, 57.6–86.3% of the declared THC ended up in the oil, and that range already includes decarb losses. Using those as a model, 1 g of 20% flower (weighed before decarb), decarbed and infused into one stick of butter (1/2 cup, 8 tbsp), is estimated at roughly 14.4–21.6 mg of THC per tablespoon, about 18.8 mg in the middle. Actual strength can fall outside this range; it is a model, not a measurement. Double the flower and you double the estimate. Plan your servings around the high number, not the low one.

Heads up: 10 mg is the adult-use per-serving cap in Ohio and Michigan and the medical cap in Florida; Ohio medical allows 55 mg and Michigan medical 50 mg per serving [2][3][4]. Homemade food isn’t lab-tested, so every number here is an estimate. Health Canada says it can take up to 4 hours to feel the full effects [25].

What is the right cannabutter ratio?

There isn’t one. The cannabutter ratio is just a dial for strength. Recipes online vary widely. One well-known magazine test used half an ounce of flower in a cup of clarified butter [5]. What you actually want is a number: how many milligrams of THC sit in each tablespoon. Once you know that, every recipe becomes simple arithmetic.

The math starts with the label. One percent of anything by weight is 10 mg per gram, so a gram of 20% Total THC flower carries 200 mg. Some of that is lost when you decarb, and some stays behind in the plant material when you strain. The table below runs those losses for the ratios people search for most.

Why three efficiency columns instead of one

Because we found no published study that gives a single right number for home butter. The best measured data come from Italian pharmacies, which make and test cannabis olive oil. Across 4,774 of those oils, 57.6% to 86.3% of the THC declared for the flower ended up in the oil [1]. Those endpoints are a range across study groups (plant variety by preparation method), not the lowest and highest individual batches. That range includes methods that decarbed in the oven first, so it already counts decarb losses. The 75% “middle” setting is a round value near the middle of the measured range, not a measurement. And that’s oil, made by trained staff with lab checks, not butter in a home kitchen. A Steep Hill lab scientist quoted by Leafly estimated 40–60% for dairy butter, and another expert guessed closer to 30%, but those are opinions, not measurements [6].

You’ll also see two popular shortcuts: “assume 70% infusion efficiency” and “multiply by 0.62.” We looked for a primary source for both and found none [6]. For comparison, this calculator’s factor is 0.75 at the middle setting and runs from 0.576 to 0.863 across the range, so 0.62 sits inside it. It just isn’t a measured number.

How strong is my cannabutter per tablespoon?

Here’s the whole calculation in words, the same one the calculator runs:

Cannabutter strength, step by step

grams × Total THC % × 10 = mg of THC on the label × efficiency (0.576 / 0.75 / 0.863) = mg in the butter (decarb losses included) ÷ tablespoons of butter = mg per tablespoon (÷ 3 for teaspoons)

Worked example: 1 g × 20 × 10 = 200 mg × 0.75 = 150 mg in 1/2 cup (8 tbsp) of butter = 18.8 mg per tablespoon, 6.3 mg per teaspoon. Skipped the oven decarb? Then this model can’t estimate your butter; here’s why.

Why there’s no separate decarb factor. The pharmacy study measured efficiency by comparing the THC in each finished oil with the THC declared for the flower that went in, and its range includes methods that decarbed the flower in the oven first [1]. Decarb losses are already inside 57.6–86.3%, so multiplying by a decarb loss on top would count them twice and make the butter look weaker than it may be. For the chemistry: in a lab test on a cannabis extract in a vacuum oven, converting the THCA at 230°F (110°C) lost 7.94% of the THCA plus THC [7]. In a lab study, hemp flower heated for two hours lost roughly 6% to 31% of its total CBD at 140–302°F (60–150°C), more at the hotter end [8]. Those are reasons a home batch may come out below the pharmacy range, not factors the calculator applies.

Why leaning high is the safe direction. Underestimate your butter and every brownie is stronger than planned. Overestimate it and you’re less likely to be surprised. That’s why the calculator’s color bands and reverse mode use the high column. Even the high column isn’t a ceiling: actual strength can fall outside the modeled range.

Reading Total THC on a lab label

Raw flower is mostly THCA, which loses some weight as heat turns it into THC. Ohio and Michigan labs fold that in for you. Ohio’s Division of Cannabis Control uses Delta-9 THC + Delta-8 THC + (0.877 × THCA) = Total THC. The state’s own example has 0.20% THC, 0.11% Delta-8 and 22.70% THCA adding up to 20.22% Total THC [9]. Michigan uses the same 0.877 factor [10]. So if you’re in Ohio or Michigan, type the Total THC number straight into the calculator and don’t multiply it again.

Florida readers: we couldn’t confirm which formula Florida labels use, so check whether yours says “Total THC.” If it lists only THCA and THC, do the conversion yourself. Our Strain Finder pulls the THC and terpene numbers posted on real Ohio, Michigan and Florida dispensary menus, so you can see how a strain’s THC is reported before you shop.

How do I make cannabutter on the stove?

You need a baking sheet, a small saucepan, a thermometer if you have one, and cheesecloth or a fine strainer.

Stovetop cannabutter

  1. Decarb in the oven first. Break the flower up and spread it thin on parchment. Italian pharmacy procedures call for a layer no more than 5 mm (about 1/5 inch) thick, ideally 1–2 mm [11]. Bake at 240°F (115°C) for 40 minutes in a thin layer. In a lab test on a cannabis extract in a vacuum oven, THCA was almost all gone after 30 minutes at 230°F [7]; flower in a home oven is likely slower. The pharmacy procedure uses 115°C (239°F) for 40 minutes [11]. Don’t push past 300°F. Bigger losses are measured from about 250°F (120°C) up, and they grow with time and temperature [8]. You may read that THC boils off at 315°F (157°C); a 2023 review of cannabinoid physical properties says that boiling point is wrong [12]. Our decarb temperature chart has the full time-and-temperature breakdown.
  2. Melt the butter with water. Put the butter in the saucepan with enough water that there’s always a layer under the melted fat. Use the lowest heat your stove has.
  3. Add the decarbed flower and hold it at a gentle simmer, about 212°F (100°C). The pharmacy procedures heated the oil in a container set in an external boiling-water bath at 100°C [11]. Adding water straight to the pan is a home adaptation of that, not what the study measured. We found no extraction data below 212°F, so a lower setting is an untested assumption. Small bubbles are fine. Keep it from a rolling, splattering boil.
  4. Give it 30 to 60 minutes, stirring now and then. In pharmacy oil tests, 30 minutes at 212°F pulled out as much THC as 60 or 120 minutes [11]. One standard pharmacy method uses 40 minutes in a boiling-water bath [11].
  5. Strain through cheesecloth or a fine mesh strainer into a heat-safe container. Press gently.
  6. Chill until the butter sets on top. Lift the butter off and pour away the water underneath.
  7. Measure what you got back and enter that amount in the calculator. Some fat always stays in the plant material, so the strained butter is usually a little less than you started with. The efficiency range was measured as a concentration in pharmacy oils, so applying it to the butter you recover adds uncertainty; one study of pharmacy oils noted that no study had tracked losses through the whole preparation [16]. Then label the container with the date and the estimated mg per tablespoon.

Why the water? In the pharmacy procedures, the container of oil sat in an external bath of boiling water, at 100°C (212°F), after an oven decarb [11]. Adding water to the pan is a home adaptation. A water bath or added water keeps the mixture near 212°F (100°C) in practice, but it was not what the study measured, and we found no published study comparing a water bath with direct heat for potency. The water also helps keep the milk solids in butter from scorching on the bottom of the pan. You’ll read that the water “washes out chlorophyll” or bitterness. We found no study behind that.

How to make strong cannabutter

The honest answer: use more flower, or stronger flower, per cup of butter, and decarb it properly. Strength scales in a straight line with the grams you put in. The pharmacy tests used up to 200 g of flower per liter of oil, which is about 47 g per US cup, and doubling the flower from 100 to 200 g per liter roughly doubled the THC that ended up in the oil [11]. That’s far more flower than a home recipe uses, so a home batch is unlikely to hit a ceiling where the butter stops taking more.

One thing to keep in mind about strong butter: it makes a teaspoon a big serving. If you make a strong batch, do the math in teaspoons, or plan to use less of it in each recipe.

Crockpot and sous-vide cannabutter

Crockpot cannabutter

Crock-Pot says its slow cookers reach a simmer and stabilize at about 209°F on both High and Low [13]. That’s just under boiling and close to the 212°F the pharmacy procedures use, so the steps are the same as the stovetop method: decarb in the oven first, add butter, water and flower, then strain and chill. It’s steady and hands-off. The common advice to run a crockpot for 8 hours or more doesn’t have a measured payoff. In the pharmacy tests, extra time past 30 minutes at 212°F didn’t add THC [11].

Sous-vide cannabutter

A sous-vide circulator holds temperature precisely, with the butter and flower sealed in a bag or jar. The pharmacy data are at 212°F and we found no extraction data below that, so any lower setpoint is a guess. We also found no published potency data for sous-vide cannabutter. In one decarb study (we could read only its abstract), keeping oxygen out reduced the loss of active cannabinoids at high temperatures [14], which is a reason to think a sealed bag might help. We found no published study that measured it.

What butter should I use for cannabutter?

Use the butter you’d bake with. Here’s what the evidence supports on the best butter to use for cannabutter:

  • Butterfat. By federal law, anything sold as butter in the US is at least 80% milk fat [15]. The rest is mostly water and milk solids. THC dissolves in the fat, so a higher-fat butter puts a bit more fat in each tablespoon. We found no published study of whether that changes how much THC it takes up.
  • Salted vs. unsalted. The legal definition of butter allows salt either way [15]. We found no evidence that salt changes potency. Unsalted is handy because you can salt the recipe to taste.
  • Clarified butter or ghee. Clarifying removes the water and milk solids, which leaves almost pure fat and nothing to scorch. The magazine test mentioned above used clarified butter [5]. We couldn’t find an official standard for ghee’s fat percentage, so we won’t quote one.
  • European-style, grass-fed or high-fat butter. No study we found shows any of these makes stronger cannabutter.

Comparing fats more broadly, a study of medical cannabis oils found no difference in cannabinoid levels between olive oil and MCT oil over 90 days, though MCT oil pulled more terpenes [16].

Does longer simmering make stronger cannabutter?

Not according to the only controlled test we found. When pharmacy researchers held cannabis oil at 212°F for 30, 60 and 120 minutes, the amount of THC they got out didn’t differ in any meaningful way [11].

What does matter is the decarb. The same pharmacy data show that oils made by simmering raw flower at around 212°F (100°C), with no oven step, stayed 50–90% in the inactive acid form. Methods that decarbed in the oven first came out 93–99% active THC [1]. The magazine butter test lines up: its water-simmered batch tested 5.18 mg/g THC with 6.22 mg/g still as THCA, while its best batch was 9.58 mg/g THC with almost no THCA left [5]. One batch each, not a study, but it points the same way: hours on the stove don’t replace 30–40 minutes in the oven.

What about lecithin? We found no controlled study of lecithin in homemade edibles, and a review of oral THC found absorption highly variable across product types [17]. In that magazine test the lecithin butter actually tested lower than the best batch (6.84 vs. 9.58 mg/g THC) [5]. The calculator gives it no bonus.

How do I size cannabutter to a brownie box?

The easiest way to get cannabutter brownies right: make exactly as much cannabutter as the box calls for in fat, use all of it, and cut the number of brownies the box says. Then each brownie’s strength depends only on the flower and the number of pieces. Melted cannabutter can stand in for oil one-for-one by volume. Butter is about 80% fat [15], so you’re adding a little less fat than the oil would, and the texture may change slightly.

Cannabis oil instead of butter: the no-dairy path

No dairy? Make cannabis oil instead. The method is the same: decarb in the oven, hold the oil at about 212°F (the pharmacy procedures set the oil in an external boiling-water bath [11]; a double boiler is the closest home setup, though it isn’t what the study measured), strain, and store. The math is the same too. In fact, the efficiency range in this calculator comes from olive oil, not butter [1].

  • Olive oil: what the pharmacy studies used, and it held its cannabinoid levels as well as MCT oil over 90 days [16].
  • MCT oil: same cannabinoid results as olive oil in that study, with more terpenes pulled into the oil [16].
  • Coconut oil: popular for baking because it’s solid at room temperature. It wasn’t compared in any study we found, so the calculator treats it like any other fat.

For brownies, oil-based box mixes take cannabis oil directly in place of the oil. King Arthur’s gluten-free box even prints a dairy-free swap: “To make non-dairy, replace butter with vegetable oil” [22]. In the calculator, enter oil in cups or tablespoons. The grams setting assumes butter at 227 g per cup.

How long does cannabutter last? Storage and shelf life

We found no published study of cannabutter shelf life, so we go by the guidance for plain butter. University extension guidance gives butter 1–2 months in the refrigerator and 6–9 months in the freezer, wrapped or covered tightly [23]. Salted butter can sit on the counter for a day or two below 70°F in a covered dish [24], but don’t do that with cannabutter. It looks just like regular butter, and the person who grabs it for toast won’t know.

On the THC side, the medical oils in one study held steady for their first 60 days in the fridge [16]. That tells you about potency, not food safety. Our habits:

  • Label every container with the date and the estimated mg per tablespoon from the calculator.
  • Freeze it in measured portions, like one tablespoon per cube in an ice cube tray, so each piece has an estimated strength, assuming thorough mixing and equal portions.
  • Store it out of reach of kids and pets, and away from the regular butter.

Cannabutter FAQ

How much weed do I need for one stick of butter?

It depends on how strong you want each tablespoon. One gram of flower labeled 20% Total THC, weighed before you decarb it, in one stick (1/2 cup, 8 tablespoons) of butter is estimated at roughly 14.4 to 21.6 mg of THC per tablespoon, about 18.8 mg in the middle estimate. That is a modeled estimate using pharmacy olive-oil group efficiencies; actual strength can fall outside this range. Two grams doubles that. Pick a target strength first, then use reverse mode in the calculator to get the grams.

Do I have to decarb weed before making cannabutter?

Yes, if you want predictable strength. In a study of pharmacy-made cannabis oils, batches simmered at around 212°F (100°C) without an oven decarb stayed 50 to 90 percent in the inactive acid form, while batches decarbed in the oven first were 93 to 99 percent active THC. [1]

Should I multiply the label THC by 0.877?

Not if your label lists Total THC. In Ohio and Michigan, Total THC is already calculated as THC plus 0.877 times THCA, so the conversion is built in. Only apply 0.877 yourself if your label lists THCA by itself. [9][10]

Is the 70 percent cannabutter infusion rule real?

We could not find a primary source for it, or for the 0.62 factor some recipes use. The best measured data come from 4,774 pharmacy-made cannabis olive oils, where 57.6 to 86.3 percent of the declared THC made it into the oil. That is a range across study groups, not individual batches. The calculator lets you pick the low, middle or high end of that range. [1][6]

How long does cannabutter last in the fridge?

We found no published study of cannabutter shelf life. University extension guidance for plain butter is 1 to 2 months in the refrigerator and 6 to 9 months in the freezer, wrapped tightly. Label it clearly and keep it where nobody mistakes it for regular butter. [23]

Can I use the same math for cannabis coconut oil or olive oil?

Yes. The math is the same: grams times THC percent times 10, times the infusion efficiency, divided by the tablespoons of fat, for flower decarbed in the oven first. The measured infusion range actually comes from olive oil; coconut oil has not been measured in the studies we found. Use the cups or tablespoons setting, since the grams setting assumes butter at 227 grams per cup. [1]
