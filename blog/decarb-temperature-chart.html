Decarb Temperature Chart

How long to decarb weed, and at what temperature, from measured results and clearly labeled models and protocols. Where nobody has measured something, this page says so.

TL;DR: Grind your flower, spread it in a thin layer, and bake at 240°F (115°C) for 40 minutes. That is the setting pharmacy protocols use before making cannabis oil[4], and pharmacy oils made after it came out 93% decarboxylated THC[5]. Modeled from lab rate constants, about 95% conversion at 240°F takes 20 to 40 minutes[1]. Hotter is faster, but there’s no safe line where THC starts to evaporate: losses put down to evaporation have been reported from about 250°F (120–122°C) up, and they grow with time and temperature[10] [3]. No study shows a hot oven gets you more THC, only a shorter window. CBD-rich flower needs roughly twice as long[1].

By Blazin Bill · Published November 27, 2026

Most decarb charts online come from one person's oven or from another chart. Every time, temperature and percentage on this one comes from a published study or white paper, a pharmacy protocol, state rule text or a clearly labeled vendor test, or is calculated from one of those and marked “modeled.” The bracketed numbers link to the sources at the bottom. I'm a writer, not a chemist, so this page reports what the papers measured and says where they stop.

One thing to know up front: the cleanest kinetics data comes from cannabis extract heated in a vacuum oven[1], not ground flower on a sheet pan in your kitchen. Flower brings moisture, plant material and oxygen along with it, so expect home results to run a little slower and lose a little more than the lab numbers.

Decarb temperature chart: what temperature decarbs THCA into THC?

Raw flower is mostly THCA, the acid form. Heat knocks a carboxyl group off as CO2, and what's left is THC. The reaction runs faster the hotter it gets. In one study, it didn't finish within an hour below 212°F (100°C)[1]. Here's what each study found, coolest to hottest.

The model rows use the 2016 study's rate constants[1]; the ranges span its fitted curve and its direct measurements. A 2020 study of hemp flower heated in an oven from 176°F to 320°F (80–160°C) confirmed the same basic behavior, with THCA decarboxylating fastest of the cannabinoid acids[2]. Its full rate table is paywalled, and I haven't seen it.

How long does it take to decarb weed at 240°F, 250°F, or 300°F?

240°F (115°C): 40 minutes

This is the setting with the most real-world evidence. An Italian pharmacy protocol spreads flower "in a thin layer (maximum 5 mm, preferably 1–2 mm)" and bakes it at 115°C for 40 minutes[4]. In lab tests of 4,774 pharmacy-made cannabis oils, the ones made after this step came out 93% neutral THC[5]. The kinetics model agrees: about 95% conversion in 20–40 minutes and 99% in 30–60[1]. Going a few minutes past 40 is still inside the modeled range for 99%.

250°F (121°C): 20 to 40 minutes

Modeled at about 95% in 13–26 minutes and 99% in 20–40 minutes[1]. No study measured THC loss at 250°F over a 30–40 minute decarb, though losses put down to evaporation have been reported at 252°F (122°C)[10]. No study measured CBN formation from flower at 250°F over a normal decarb, so the online claim that 250°F "burns THC into CBN" isn’t backed by home-oven data.

300°F (149°C): minutes, and that's the problem

The model says 95% in 2–5 minutes[1]. The trouble is what comes after. At 293°F (145°C), THC peaked at 7 minutes and was halved by 40[6]. The hotter pharmacy protocol, 284°F (140°C) for 30 minutes, left less of the cannabinoids in acid form than the 240°F setting[4]. In the big pharmacy dataset, a 293°F (145°C) oven step followed by ultrasound extraction gave 99% neutral THC, against 93% for 240°F[5]. Both papers report how much was left in acid form; neither concludes which setting keeps more total THC.

How to decarb weed in the oven

  1. Put an oven thermometer on the rack and preheat to 240°F (115°C). Go by the thermometer, not the dial.
  2. Weigh the flower before you decarb it. That's the number your dose math uses.
  3. Grind it and spread it on parchment as thin as you can: no more than 5 mm deep, ideally 1–2 mm[4]. One 2026 study using finely ground flower in a thin bed measured rates about twice those reported elsewhere, and the authors suggest the grind and bed depth may be why[3].
  4. Bake 40 minutes. For CBD-dominant flower, see the CBD section below.
  5. Let it cool, then go straight to your butter or oil.

Where the "88% conversion" number comes from

You'll see "240°F converts about 88% of THCA" all over the internet. No study we could find measured that. The number lines up with 0.877, the molecular-weight factor regulators use to turn THCA into THC on a label[11]:

THC (314.47 g/mol) ÷ THCA (358.48 g/mol) = 0.877

That 12.3% is the CO2 leaving the molecule. It isn't THCA that failed to convert. The kinetics model says conversion at 240°F for 40 minutes is near-complete[1]. The only lab-tested 240°F/40-minute flower sample we found, a single vendor test, kept 84.5% of its maximum potential THC, and that one number can't tell you how much was unconverted and how much was lost[14].

What happens if you decarb weed too hot or too long?

Two things can happen to THC once it's formed: it can oxidize into CBN, or it can evaporate. At oven temperatures, evaporation turns out to be the bigger risk.

  • In the dark, under vacuum, no significant CBN formed. In the 2016 extract study, Wang observed no significant oxidation of THC to CBN under its dark, vacuum-oven conditions, and pure THCA heated at 230°F (110°C) for 40 minutes produced only THC[1]. That is one experiment with oxygen and light kept out, not a rule for a kitchen oven. The total THCA + THC in the extract fell by 7.94% by the end of the reaction[1].
  • At vaporizer heat, CBN shows up. Hemp flower sealed in small vials at 446°F (230°C) for 12 minutes ended up with more than 3% CBN in a THC-rich variety, and a THC isolate lost about 13% of its THC to CBN[3].
  • Time at temperature costs cannabinoids. The same 2026 study held hemp flower at each temperature for 120 minutes. Total CBD fell 8.3% at 140°F (60°C), 5.9% at 194°F (90°C), 18.3% at 248°F (120°C) and 31.3% at 302°F (150°C), and the authors describe the evaporative loss as significant from 248°F up[3]. That hold is three times longer than a 40-minute decarb, so it isn't the same exposure, but the direction is clear.
  • Evaporation speeds up quickly. In a lab vaporizer, 10% and 20% of the THC left the flower within 20 and 40 seconds at 356°F (180°C). At 428°F (220°C) it was 34% and 50%. The evaporation rate roughly triples for every 36°F (20°C) increase[7]. The same paper says the THC “boiling point” repeated on many websites is wrong: THC’s real boiling point is far higher, and it gives off vapor at lower temperatures too, faster as it gets hotter[7]. Earlier researchers reported THC and CBD losses at 252°F and 293°F (122°C and 145°C) and put them down to evaporation[10].
  • Oxygen makes it worse. In the hemp-flower oven study, keeping oxygen out reduced the loss of THC and CBD at higher temperatures and longer times[2].

What nobody has measured is CBN formation in a home oven at 220–250°F over 30–90 minutes. All the CBN numbers above come from 446°F (230°C). The longest low-temperature losses are 120-minute holds, three times longer than a 40-minute decarb. So "a little too long at 240°F" probably costs you something, but there's no study that puts a number on it.

Does CBD (CBDA) decarb at the same temperature as THC?

Same temperatures, slower clock. In the 2016 extract study, THCA's reaction rate was "always approximately twice" that of CBDA at each temperature where it measured rate constants[1]. Using its rate constants, we calculate about 92 minutes for 99% CBDA conversion at 230°F (110°C), against about 42 minutes for THCA[1]. CBDA also loses more to side reactions: 18.05% of total CBDA + CBD at 230°F (110°C) and 25.2% at 266°F (130°C), compared with about 8% for THCA[1].

How do I decarb flower, kief, hash, concentrates, and AVB?

This is where the evidence is thinnest. No peer-reviewed study compares kief, hash or concentrates with flower as a decarb variable, and none isolates moisture content. About AVB potency: in the lab vaporizer test, flower that started at 14.5% total THC was down to 7.10% after three minutes at 356°F (180°C)[7]. A separate study recovered 54.6–82.7% of the THC in the vapor across four electric vaporizers[8]. Nobody has measured what is left after a real home session, so treat AVB potency as unknown, not as reliably low.

How much decarbed weed do I use in brownies?

Start from the label, not the decarbed weight. Ohio's lab-testing guidance defines total THC as delta-9 THC + delta-8 THC + (0.877 × THCA)[11]. That means the CO2 lost in decarb is already built into the label's total THC number. Don't multiply by 0.877 again, and don't re-weigh the flower after the oven. Whether you shop Ohio adult-use or medical, read the total THC line. Florida's rule text we checked doesn't spell out a formula, so if your Florida medical label lists THC and THCA separately, use the 0.877 factor on the THCA yourself. When you're picking flower in the Strain Finder or at the counter, the number that matters for baking is total THC, not THCA alone.

The arithmetic

1% THC = 10 mg per gram 20% total THC = 200 mg/g; 200 mg/g × 3.5 g = 700 mg potential

Not all of that ends up in your butter. In olive oil made by pharmacies, extraction efficiency for THC ranged from 57.6% to 86.3% across preparation methods and plant varieties[5]. It was measured against the flower’s declared content, so it already covers decarb and extraction losses together: don’t take a second cut for the decarb. Nobody has measured butter, so treat this as a rough range. In another study of pharmacy olive oils, 30, 60 or 120 minutes in a 212°F (100°C) water bath made no significant difference to how much THC was recovered[4]. That finding covers the oil methods and times it tested, not butter.

700 mg × 0.576 to 0.863 = about 403 to 604 mg in the butter ÷ 16 brownies = about 25 to 38 mg each

The cannabutter ratio calculator’s default is 75%, a round value near the middle of the measured range, not a measurement. At 75% it’s about 525 mg in the butter, or about 33 mg a brownie.

That's well above the 10 mg legal serving. Ohio caps adult-use edibles at 10 mg THC per serving and 100 mg per package[12]. Florida caps a single medical serving at 10 mg and a multi-serving edible at 200 mg[13]. Ohio's medical cap is 55 mg per serving[12]. Homemade brownies aren't regulated. 10 mg is the most a single legal serving can hold in Ohio adult-use and Florida medical[12] [13], so this page uses it as the benchmark for the math. It’s a ceiling, not a recommendation. Here's that calculation in reverse:

Working backward to the 10 mg legal serving (16-piece pan)

16 × 10 mg = 160 mg needed in the butter 160 ÷ 0.863 to 0.576 = about 185 to 278 mg potential, before losses ÷ 200 mg/g (20% flower) = about 0.93 to 1.39 g of flower

Same olive-oil efficiency range as above; butter hasn’t been measured. The top of that range assumes the worst extraction. If the butter extracts as well as the best oils did (86.3%), 1.39 g would put about 15 mg in each piece, over the cap. Sizing from the high end of efficiency keeps every estimate at or under it:

160 ÷ (200 mg/g × 0.863) = about 0.93 g of 20% flower

That’s an estimate worked back from the 10 mg cap, not a dose recommendation. The calculator’s 75% default gives about 1.1 g, which would come to about 11.5 mg a piece at 86.3% efficiency.

So the cap-based estimate is about 0.93 g of 20% flower for a 16-piece pan, weighed before the decarb. Run forward, 0.93 g works out to about 6.7 to 10 mg a piece across the measured oil range, or about 8.7 mg at the calculator’s 75% default. None of those is a measured butter potency. Do I need to decarb weed for butter, tincture, capsules, or smoking?

  • Butter or oil: yes. Pharmacy oils made by simmering raw flower in oil at about 212°F (100°C), with no oven step first, kept 50% to 90% of their cannabinoids in the acid form[5]. The ones decarbed in the oven first came out 93% to 99% neutral THC[5].
  • Capsules: yes. A capsule is just oil or butter in a shell, so the same rule applies.
  • Tincture: yes, if you want THC. Decarb depends on heat. An alcohol soak at room temperature or a low-temperature tincture doesn't provide enough, so decarb the flower first. None of the studies here tested tinctures directly.
  • Smoking or vaping: no. The flame or the heating chamber does it in seconds. In a lab vaporizer, decarb was practically complete within 20 seconds at 428°F (220°C)[7].

If you want THC without any cooking, a dry herb vaporizer skips the oven, the butter and the wait. For how eating cannabis differs from inhaling it, see our edibles guide.

Common decarb mistakes

  1. Trusting the oven dial. Every number on this page assumes the air really is at that temperature. Use an oven thermometer.
  2. Going hot to save time. At 300°F (149°C) and above, you're working in minutes, and loss to vapor climbs as the oven gets hotter: roughly threefold per 36°F (20°C) in a lab vaporizer[7].
  3. Piling it up. Keep it under 5 mm deep[4]. A thicker bed probably heats less evenly.
  4. Skipping decarb and simmering longer instead. Pharmacy olive oils simmered at about 212°F (100°C) with no oven step stayed 50% to 90% in the acid form[5], and in a separate oil study, 60 or 120 minutes in a water bath recovered no significantly more THC than 30[4]. Butter hasn’t been tested either way.
  5. Doing the math on the decarbed weight, or applying 0.877 twice. Total THC on an Ohio label already includes the 0.877 factor[11].
  6. Giving CBD flower the THC time. CBDA converts at about half the speed[1].
  7. Treating AVB as a known quantity. In a lab vaporizer, decarb approached completion within 40 seconds at 356°F (180°C), but residual THCA was still measured[7]. Nobody has measured how much THCA or THC is left after a home session, so its potency is unknown.

Before you bake

A correct decarb makes your butter stronger than an undecarbed batch, so a recipe you've used before can hit harder than you remember. Controlled brownie studies found effects peaked 1.5 to 3 hours after eating[15] [17], and Health Canada says it can take up to 4 hours to feel the full effects[16]. Keep homemade edibles labeled and away from kids and pets.

Decarb FAQ

What is the best temperature to decarb weed in the oven?

240°F (115°C) for about 40 minutes, with the flower ground and spread in a thin layer. That is the setting pharmacy protocols use before making cannabis oil[4]. Modeled from lab rate constants, about 95% conversion at 240°F takes 20 to 40 minutes[1]. Use an oven thermometer, because the dial is only a suggestion.

Can you decarb weed at 300°F to save time?

You can, but the window is short. Modeled from lab rate constants, 300°F (149°C) gets about 95% conversion in 2 to 5 minutes[1]. There is no single temperature where THC starts to evaporate. The THC boiling point repeated on many websites is wrong, according to a 2023 paper[7], and losses put down to evaporation have been reported from about 250°F (120–122°C) up, growing with time and temperature[10] [3]. Hemp flower held at 302°F (150°C) for 120 minutes lost 31% of its total CBD[3]. No study shows a fast decarb ends up with more THC than a slower one at 240°F.

Is a sealed mason jar better than an open tray for decarbing?

Nobody has measured it. Keeping oxygen out reduced cannabinoid losses in an oven study of hemp flower[2], which argues for a sealed container, but a glass jar also takes longer to heat through. If you use a jar, start timing once the jar itself is up to temperature.

Do I need to decarb kief or hash before making butter?

Yes, if it has not been heated. Simmering alone did not finish the job in the oils that were tested: pharmacy olive oils made from flower at about 212°F (100°C) without an oven decarb stayed 50% to 90% in the acid form[5]. No study has tested kief, hash or butter, so using the flower setting is this page's unmeasured extrapolation, not a measured setting.

Do I need to decarb already vaped bud (AVB)?

The lab data suggests most of the decarb is already done, but home sessions haven't been measured. In a lab vaporizer test, decarb approached completion: THCA in the flower fell to 1.4% or less within 40 seconds at 356°F (180°C), though that residual THCA was still measured[7]. In the same test, total THC in the leftover material fell steadily after the first 20 seconds. Nobody has measured how much THCA or THC is left after a real home session, so AVB potency is unknown, not reliably low.

How much weight does weed lose when you decarb it?

The chemistry accounts for only a few percent. The CO2 that leaves is 12.3% of the THCA's mass[11], which is about 2.5% of the weight of 20% THCA flower. Anything beyond that is mostly water, and no study has measured home decarb weight loss. Weigh your flower before you decarb and do your dose math on that weight.
