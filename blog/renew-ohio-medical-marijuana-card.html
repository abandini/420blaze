How to Renew Your Ohio Medical Marijuana Card (and Get Your Doctor’s Updated Recommendation Onto It)

By Blazin Bill • September 27, 2026 • 9 min read • Cleveland

Quick Answer

Your Ohio medical card has two clocks: your physician’s recommendation and your state registration. The registration is good for one year and expires on the last day of the month it was issued; the state emails you 45 days out, and you can renew up to 30 days before it expires. Renewing is two steps: (1) see a physician with a Certificate to Recommend — in person or by telehealth — who enters the renewed recommendation in the Registry from their account; (2) log in at ohiomedicalmarijuanaregistry.com, open the Card tab, click Renew Card, accept, continue, download. The state fee is $0 since May 15, 2024. A new recommendation you don’t activate within 90 days is voided. There is no “rec card” — adult use has no card at all. And if you let the card lapse, the rule says you have seven days to dispose of what you have.

Checked against the Ohio Administrative Code, the Ohio Revised Code, the State Medical Board’s rules and the Registry’s own help center on September 27, 2026. Every rule below cites its section. I write about cannabis; I am not a lawyer or a clinician, and the Registry changes its screens more often than the rules change. If a screen doesn’t match this page, the rule still does.

Ohio’s medical program didn’t go away when adult use arrived; it moved. Since March 20, 2026 (Senate Bill 56) both programs live in Chapter 3796 of the Revised Code under one regulator, the Division of Cannabis Control (DCC) in the Department of Commerce, and the medical registration rules live in Ohio Administrative Code Chapter 1301:18-10. Most of what turns up in a search for “renew Ohio medical card” is a telehealth clinic’s booking page, and a surprising number of them still say the state fee is $50. It isn’t. Here is the whole process, with the rule for each step.

First: there is no “rec card”

People ask how to renew their “rec card” because other states have one. Ohio doesn’t. An adult-use consumer is simply anyone 21 or older (ORC 3796.01), and you buy adult-use product with a government photo ID and nothing else. There is nothing to register and nothing to renew. The only card in Ohio cannabis is the medical marijuana registry identification card issued to registered patients and caregivers under ORC 3796.08, and that is the card the rest of this page is about. If you have been buying adult-use and think your “card” expired, it didn’t; you never had one. The rules for adult-use buying, possession and use are in our Ohio weed laws explainer.

The two clocks

This is the part that trips people up, because the two dates rarely line up.

The card itself prints both: a registration issued date, a registration expiration date and a separate recommendation expiration date

Three deadlines hang off those two dates:

  • 45 days before the card expires, the Division sends you a notice (1301:18-10-01(H)(2)). It comes by email from the Registry, so make sure that address is current and check spam.
  • 30 days before is the earliest the physician’s renewal submission can go in (1301:18-10-01(H)(3)); your own click on Renew Card comes after that. The Registry’s help center puts it the same way: you may renew “within 30 days of registration expiration (regardless of recommendation status).” The Medical Board’s rule for physicians mirrors it: when your registration “will expire within thirty days,” the physician or their delegate submits the renewal (OAC 4731-32-03(D)(1)).
  • 90 days after a recommendation is written, a registration submission that hasn’t been completed goes stale (1301:18-10-01(D)) — the rule is written for registration submissions, new or renewal, and the Registry applies it in plain terms. Its activation email is blunt: complete your registration within 90 days “or your recommendation will be voided, and you will need to obtain a new recommendation from your physician.”

What happens if you miss it. “Failure to renew a patient registration will result in an automatic expiration of the registration card” (1301:18-10-01(H)(4)) — and then the part nobody reads: “Patients shall dispose of all medical cannabis within seven calendar days of the expiration of their registration” (H)(5). An expired card is not a grace period. A dispensary can’t sell to you, the possession protection in ORC 3796.22 belongs to a registered patient, and the rule gives you seven days to dispose of what you have — not to use it up, and not to sell or hand it to anyone. Renew early.

Renewal, step by step

  1. Look up both dates. Log in at ohiomedicalmarijuanaregistry.com — note the spelling. As of September 2026 the Division’s own renewal page tells patients to sign in at a “medicalcannabisregistry” address that doesn’t resolve; the working registry, the one every other DCC page links, is the one above. Passwords there expire every 90 days and sessions time out after 20 idle minutes, so a “password expired” message is normal — use the reset link. Note the recommendation’s “Expires” date and the card’s expiration.
  2. Book the physician visit early, timed so the renewal lands inside the 30-day window. The Medical Board rule tells the physician to submit the renewal when your registration “will expire within thirty days,” and the Division only accepts renewal submissions inside that window — so the visit can happen earlier, but the submission can’t, and you haven’t renewed anything until it’s in and you’ve clicked Renew Card. Only a physician holding a Certificate to Recommend from the State Medical Board can renew you (ORC 3796.08; ORC 4731.30; OAC 4731-32-02). Under Medical Board rule 4731-32-03(A), the required physician-patient relationship can be established and maintained “through an examination of the patient by the physician either in-person or through the use of telehealth services,” so a video visit is fine unless your physician prefers otherwise. The physician has to confirm your qualifying condition, pull your prescription-monitoring (OARRS) report covering the last 12 months, and document risks and benefits (4731-32-03(C)); that’s why the visit isn’t instant. Their fee is private and unregulated — shop around.
  3. The physician submits the renewal, not you. The statute puts the application in the physician’s hands: the physician “or the physician’s delegate shall submit the application on the patient’s or caregiver’s behalf” (ORC 3796.08(A)(1)), from the physician’s own Registry account, marked as a renewal (1301:18-10-01(C)(3)). You will get an automatic email when it lands; for a first registration it comes from no-reply-pmpaware@globalnotifications.com with the subject line “ACTION NEEDED: Ohio MMCP Registry - Account Registration,” which is exactly the kind of message a spam filter eats.
  4. Renew the card. The Division’s renewal page states the timing plainly: renew “within 30 days before your registration expires, or after you receive a new recommendation if your registration already expired,” and “if both your registration and recommendation are expired, you must get a new recommendation before you can renew. After the new recommendation is issued, the RENEW CARD button will appear.” Back in the Registry: Registry Details → Card tab → RENEW CARD. Read and Accept the legal statements, check the summary page, click Continue. That’s the whole transaction. The fee is $0. The Division’s cannabis FAQ says it in one sentence — “There is no activation or renewal cost associated with state registration” — after it dropped the $50 patient / $25 caregiver fee to one cent on March 4, 2024 and removed it entirely on May 15, 2024 (“patients and caregivers will no longer need to submit payment… when activating a new registration or renewing an existing registration”). The Division’s caregiver renewal page still says “after you pay the renewal fee”; it’s stale. If a clinic or an old guide tells you to pay the state $50, it is out of date; if a website that isn’t the Registry asks for a “state fee,” close it.
  5. Download the new card. It appears on the left of your profile with the new expiration date. Click Download and save the PDF or PNG; nothing is mailed. You must carry your registry identification whenever you have medical cannabis, and show the card plus “valid, unexpired government issued photographic identification” at the counter (1301:18-10-01(E)). An expired driver’s license will stop a sale even with a perfect card.

Getting your doctor’s updated approval onto the card

This is the question we get most, and the answer is that the card doesn’t carry the approval; the Registry does. The downloadable card proves you’re registered. Everything your physician decided — the qualifying condition, the dates, the number of 90-day refills, any instructions for use — lives in the Recommendation section of your Registry profile, and the dispensary verifies your eligibility, limits and any physician restrictions from the Registry, not from your printout. So when a physician issues a new recommendation, extends one, changes your condition, adds instructions, or adds or removes a caregiver, three things are true:

  • Only the physician can enter it. Patients can’t create, edit or extend a recommendation, and caregivers “are added or edited by your physician.” If your doctor wrote something on paper and told you to “update the state,” nothing will happen until their office enters it in the Registry.
  • A new recommendation does not renew the card by itself. The Registry’s help center describes the exact state you’ll see: “once you have verified that your recommendation has been renewed you may see that your card is listed as ‘Inactive’… This means that you will need to renew your card by paying the annual card renewal fee” — that article predates the fee’s elimination; the fee has been $0 since May 15, 2024, but the Inactive-until-you-click part is still exactly right. Recommendation first, then your click on Renew Card. Two clocks, two actions.
  • Check that it changed. After the office says it’s done, log in and confirm the Recommendation section shows the new Written/Issued/Expires dates and the right condition, then confirm the Card tab shows Active with the new date. “Recommendation History” shows every prior one, which is how you catch a renewal that was entered under an old, expired recommendation instead of a new one.

The 90-day supply is the other thing your doctor controls. Your recommendation is divided into two 45-day fill periods (since the March 24, 2026 rule the first fill period of a new recommendation is 46 days; the Registry’s older help article still says 45), and you can buy up to a 45-day supply in each, whenever in the period you shop; what you don’t buy doesn’t roll over (OAC 1301:18-10-03(C)). The Registry’s “Purchase Summary” and the DCC’s Patient Fill Period Calculator show where you are. A physician can also limit the “amount, form, and manner” you may buy (1301:18-10-03(B)) and, in the Division’s words, “may make additional notes on a patient’s Registry profile regarding the types or amounts of products” that “patients and dispensaries must adhere to” — so if the counter says your recommendation blocks a product, that’s a conversation with the doctor, not the budtender. In the Division’s words, “each 90-day fill period is established based on the issuance date of the patient’s active CTR physician recommendation,” which is another reason the dates matter.

When the card doesn’t update

In rough order of how often each one is the real problem:

  • The recommendation was never submitted. Ask the office to confirm the submission from the physician’s (or delegate’s) Registry account, and to read you the “Issued” and “Expires” dates they see. Don’t send anyone your Registry password; they don’t need it.
  • It was submitted for the wrong person. Legal name, date of birth, state ID number and email all have to match what the Division has. A typo creates a second, empty profile, and you never see the recommendation. The help center says to submit a support ticket with your name, date of birth, driver’s license or state ID number, the email on the account and a description of what’s wrong; Bamboo Health, the Registry vendor, researches it. Don’t create a new account to work around it — the Registry has a whole article about duplicate accounts on one email.
  • The recommendation is there but the card is Inactive. You haven’t clicked Renew Card. See step 4.
  • It’s more than 90 days old. The Registry treats it as voided. You need a new recommendation.
  • Your account is deactivated. Only the Registry administrator at the Division can activate or deactivate accounts; the vendor’s support team can’t. Email MMCPRegistry@com.ohio.gov. The Division’s toll-free line for program questions is 1-833-464-6627 (1-833-4OH-MMCP); for technical trouble with the Registry site itself, the Medical Board points to the vendor’s help desk at 1-833-276-0100.
  • The physician decided you no longer qualify. Medical Board rule 4731-32-03(F) requires a physician to terminate, or decline to renew, a recommendation if the qualifying diagnosis or symptoms are gone, the physician’s own certificate has lapsed, the physician judges that the patient or caregiver is abusing or diverting, or the patient has died. If that happens, DCC rule 1301:18-10-04(E) gives you 14 days to notify the Division and lets you request a hearing under Chapter 119 to keep the registration active — a hearing about your registration with the Division, not a way to make the doctor change their mind.

Also worth knowing: you’re required to tell the Division about any change to your registration information — address, name, email — within 30 days (1301:18-10-04(A)), and a lost card or an account someone else accessed within 10 days (10-04(B)).

Checking your physician’s certificate

A recommendation from a physician whose Certificate to Recommend has lapsed is worthless, and the physician is required to stop recommending the moment it does (4731-32-03(F)(2)). Two places to check: the State Medical Board’s CTR map and practice-location list of physicians holding a certificate, and the state’s eLicense lookup for the physician’s license status and any discipline. The certificate requires an active Ohio MD or DO license and an approved course (OAC 4731-32-02), renews with the physician’s license on condition of two hours of medical marijuana continuing education a year (ORC 4731.30(F); OAC 4731-32-02(C)), and the Board must tell the Division when a certificate lapses (4731-32-02(D)). The statute also caps a single written recommendation at 90 days, renewable for up to three more periods before a fresh examination is required (4731.30(D)(2)) — the “three refills, 360 days” the Division describes. Physicians also file an annual report on how medical marijuana is working for their patients (4731-32-03(H)). Questions about a certificate go to the Board at MedicalMarijuana@med.ohio.gov.

What the card lets you buy and hold

Outside your home, a patient may not hold more than 2.5 ounces of plant material and 15,000 mg of THC in products at any time — except on the drive home from the dispensary with the receipt, when the cap is 10 ounces and 60,000 mg. At your primary residence you may hold up to a 90-day supply within a 90-day period (OAC 1301:18-10-03(C); ORC 3796.22(B)). A dispensary may sell a patient up to 10 ounces of plant material and 60,000 mg of THC in a single day (OAC 1301:18-8-09(C)) — the Division describes it as “up to four days of supply during a single day transaction,” for patients whose conditions make trips hard; the adult-use daily cap is 2.5 ounces and 15,000 mg (1301:18-8-08). Medical product must stay “in the original package intended for direct customer sale with an unaltered dispensary label” until it’s used (OAC 1301:18-10-03(E)(2)), and edibles specifically must stay in the original packaging whenever not in use (ORC 3796.06(H)), and the THC concentration caps are the same at both counters: 35% for plant material and 70% for extracts (3796.06(E)). Medical flower is still vaporize-only — “the smoking or combustion of medical marijuana is prohibited” (3796.06(C)(1)) — while smoking is an adult-use privilege, one of the stranger leftovers of merging the two programs. Home grow runs the other way too: six plants per adult-use consumer, twelve per residence, is written for adult-use consumers (3796.04), not for patients as such. Under-18 patients cannot purchase at all; their caregiver does (1301:18-10-03(A)).

Is it worth keeping now that adult use exists?

For a lot of Northeast Ohio patients, yes, and the arithmetic is simple.

With the state fee at zero, keeping the card costs you the physician evaluation the renewal requires — usually one visit a year, sometimes more if the doctor wants follow-up. If you buy more than a couple of times a month, the excise tax alone pays for it. If you only want to smoke flower now and then, in a private place where that’s allowed, adult use is the simpler lane.

Caregivers and minors

A caregiver must be 21 or older and an Ohio resident (a parent of a minor patient can be 18), registered through the physician’s submission the same way a patient is, and may serve no more than two patients; a patient may designate no more than two caregivers, with hardship, hospice and same-household exceptions the Division can approve (OAC 1301:18-10-02; 1301:18-10-01(F)). Under the current rule a caregiver registration runs on the same clock as a patient’s — 45-day notice, 30-day renewal window, automatic expiration (1301:18-10-02) — and the caregiver fee is also $0. The Division’s caregiver renewal page hasn’t caught up: as of September 2026 it still mentions a renewal fee and a “within 90 days after your registration expires” window, and it describes an MMCP approval step of up to seven business days before a renewed caregiver can buy. Whether that approval step still runs isn’t something the rule answers, so a caregiver who renews on the last day should assume they may be unable to purchase for a week. A caregiver may hold up to 2.5 ounces and 15,000 mg of THC on the patient’s behalf “at any time” — the rule gives caregivers no drive-home exception; the 10-ounce transport allowance is written for patients only (1301:18-10-03(C)–(D)). Changing a caregiver mid-year goes through the recommending physician (1301:18-10-01(G)). If the patient is under 18, the caregiver makes every purchase. If the reason you’re reading this is a parent with Alzheimer’s — a qualifying condition by statute (ORC 3796.01(A)(6)) — read what the evidence actually shows before the appointment.

Questions people actually ask

How much does it cost to renew an Ohio medical marijuana card?

The state fee is $0. The Division of Cannabis Control cut the patient and caregiver registration fee from $50 and $25 to one cent on March 4, 2024, and eliminated it on May 15, 2024. You still pay your physician for the renewal visit, and that price is set by the clinic, not the state.

How early can I renew my Ohio medical marijuana card?

Up to 30 calendar days before the registration expires, under Ohio Administrative Code 1301:18-10-01(H)(3). The Division sends a notice 45 days before expiration. If you do not renew, the card expires automatically, and the rule requires you to dispose of your medical cannabis within seven days of expiration — disposal, not use, and no selling or transferring.

Is there a “rec card” for adult-use cannabis in Ohio?

No. Ohio’s adult-use program has no card; anyone 21 or older buys with a government photo ID. Only the medical program has a registration card, and only that card needs renewing.

My doctor renewed my recommendation but my card still says inactive. What do I do?

That is normal. A renewed recommendation shows in the Recommendation section, but the card stays inactive until you log in and click Renew Card on the Card tab. If the recommendation itself is not showing, ask the physician’s office to confirm they submitted it from their own Registry account with your exact legal name, date of birth and email; for data errors open a support ticket with the Registry’s help desk, and for account status email MMCPRegistry@com.ohio.gov.

Can I renew my Ohio medical marijuana recommendation by telehealth?

Yes. State Medical Board rule 4731-32-03 lets a physician establish and maintain the required physician-patient relationship through an examination conducted either in person or by telehealth that complies with the rule. Individual physicians may still prefer an office visit.

How long do I have to activate a new recommendation in the Ohio registry?

Ninety calendar days from the date the physician issued it. Under rule 1301:18-10-01(D), a complete registration must reach the Division within 90 days of the recommendation date; the Registry’s own activation email says the recommendation is voided after that and you need a new one.

Is an Ohio medical card still worth keeping now that adult use is legal?

Often, yes. Medical purchases carry ordinary sales tax only, while adult-use purchases add a 10% excise tax on top; medical patients buy on a 90-day supply system and may keep a full 90-day supply at home (away from home the medical limit is 2.5 oz and 15,000 mg); patients aged 18 to 20 can only buy medically; and Senate Bill 56 left home delivery and occupational-license protections in place for medical patients. The trade-offs: medical flower is vaporize-only, THC caps are the same, and a card gives no employment protection.
